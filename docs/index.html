Who votes for whom on the Guardian's 100 best novels

Perplexity at the fact that only women voted for Wolf Hall led me to analyze the data from the Guardian’s project. Here are a few different ways to look at the patterns and structures in the voting. Even if the patterns aren’t interesting, the interactive navigation makes this a pretty cool way to track down new things to read. You can start with books and voters you like and trace their connections to find other books you might not know of.

All books are grouped into clusters based on how they share voters. For example, the large plum dots represent books that both got lots of votes and also were chosen by a highly overlapping group of voters. These clusters emerge purely from the voting connections. They are not external categories. There is no ‘canon cluster’ or ‘post-colonial cluster.’ But, if you explore the cluster lists (you can get to them in the side panel after selecting a book) you will see that some appear to fit into coherent literary groupings, some have broad related attributes (like high prestige), and others are just ad hoc grab bags connected by a few overlapping ballots. Any coherence, if it exists, comes from the voters and their collective choices, not from the process of finding clusters.

Note the orphans. These books don’t fit into clusters, but they received enough votes to make it into the list.

Click any book to see who voted for it. Click a voter's name to highlight their picks. In Top 100 scope, the remaining books fade to gray.

The graph highlights different voting patterns that contrast how the voters chose books. You can look for lumpers vs. splitters, and canonistas vs. frontier explorers.

Voters each get 10 votes. In this graph, each voter is shown as a spoke on the wheel. Each spoke has its votes sorted by the same clusters that were derived for the book clusters.

The most concentrated votes are at the top and as you continue clockwise the ballots get less concentrated (i.e., each of those voters voted for books in more clusters). But concentrated is not the same as conventional. Take note of Nussaibah Younis, who defines her own cluster and none of whose books are in the top 100, and Saba Sams, who voted for books in 8 different clusters.

To the right, the major clusters are represented by pairs of circles showing the number of books vs. the total votes for the cluster. This shows how imbalanced the voting is. The top cluster has 30 books and gets 463 votes, way très canonical! The next cluster has more than twice the books but only about one third as many votes.

Click a voter's spoke to see what they picked. Click a cluster swatch to see the books in it. In Top 100 scope, picks that missed the cut turn gray.

About this project

I immediately clicked on Wolf Hall when I saw it in the Guardian’s list of the 100 best novels, to see who had voted for it. It is the book I would choose for number one. I was amazed to see that everyone who voted for it was a woman. Sad, but true, like WTF! This made me very curious about who voted for whom and if there were obvious gender trends in the voting. I used Claude AI to analyze all of the data from the Guardian’s list and to make this visualization.

This turns out to be a great way to find books. You can navigate from books that you like to voters who selected them and then to the other books that those voters also chose. Or look at voter rankings to find interesting voters. Just click around in the graphs or in the lists.

The book clusters and voter clusters are properties that emerge directly from the data, how votes overlap (or don’t) leads to the clusters. The meaning of the clusters is up for debate, but some certainly seem to have coherent identities (maybe with some fuzzy edges) while others simply share an audience for a grab bag of otherwise unrelated books.

The gender graph is an attempt to use external categories to lump the data. It is interesting, but I’m not sure it added much to the obvious fact that men mostly voted for male authors.

I looked at the ‘gender’ of books two ways. First, what percentage of ballots are for the opposite gender (non-binary voters are in all of the lists and rankings but not in these male/female statistics). Second, I thought maybe some books would be more stereotypically ‘male’ or ‘female’ and that might drive voting preferences differently from the author’s gender. Maybe male voters just prefer ‘male’ books, you know, spies, war, mayhem, exuberant misogyny and all the rest. I certainly love and admire lots of those books. Maybe women just don’t write as many books like that. So, I had Claude code all of the books as more male or more female using stereotypical, subjective interpretations (including subject matter and aspects of writing style).

It turns out that coding the books as male/female shifts the results a few points, but men’s preference for male authors easily trumps their preference for male-coded subjects.

Some of the coolest results are in the voter rankings. I have ranked voters by how clustered or diverse their ballots are, how canonical or niche, how obscure or popular their choices are. These rankings also switch around in interesting ways when you include all 700 or so books vs. just the top 100.

Nota bene, there are some tangled, intractable issues here. There is a phantom premise that haunts these kinds of comparisons, that the universe of books to vote for is not already skewed by gender or other factors. Obviously that’s wrong. It’s a real garden of forking paths. So the stats are clear, these voters voted this way in this sample. But, the ground is less solid than you might want for building a broader analysis.

To be clear, I think that to get different lists of top books, we just need to curate different collections of voters, not expect anyone in particular to have different preferences. The most important result, however, remains clear: Hilary Mantel was robbed.

I wrote this introduction old school, typing in a text editor. The rest was written and programmed by Claude. Knowing a tiny bit about how LLMs work, I suspect there is a good chance this entire analysis is completely, fraudulently wrong. But, it is wonderfully plausible which is all we really can get in the post-truth reality.
